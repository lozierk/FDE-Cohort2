In the reference, a moment is an anchor point plus a 15 s proximity window used only for grouping hits; nothing persists. It uses no LLM at ingest. I kept its RRF and its eval discipline and changed where the moment is built.

The baseline cuts fixed 120-word chunks and ignores sentence and story boundaries.

Answer
claude-sonnet-5

Chunks in time order, each prefixed [n] (mm:ss–mm:ss).

Answer rules, shared by both pipelines

  • Cite every claim with [n] and a timestamp.
  • No outside knowledge; the excerpts only.
  • Every call logs tokens, seconds and dollars.

The designed weakness

  • Lessons run about 300 words, chunks 120.
  • A lesson's story and its moral land in different chunks.
  • Top 5 buys coverage with off-topic words.
Source: README.md "Pipelines"; DESIGN.md "Baseline"
The baseline is deliberately a fair, ordinary chunk RAG: the same embedder, store and answer model as the moment side. The chunker ignores sentence boundaries on purpose, because the point is to see what cutting mid-idea costs.

Embedding drift proposes boundaries; one Haiku pass turns them into 14 labelled moments.

4 · Moment RAG implementation · retrieval

RRF over two collections plus an adaptive cut returns 1.92 moments per question.

How a query runs

  • Embed the question once.
  • Query summaries and full text, top 10 each.
  • Fuse with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, k=60.
  • Always keep the top moment.
  • Keep moments 2–3 only if score ≥ 0.97 × top.
  • Sonnet reads full moment text, labelled [n] Title (mm:ss–mm:ss).

5 · Comparison · how it was scored

Twelve questions in three types test single facts, story-plus-moral, and cross-lesson answers.

span · 4

Needs the story and its moral

"What happened to a student who failed the uniform inspection, and what does McRaven say that teaches?"

multi · 4

Spans two or three lessons

"What do the night swim and the underwater ship attack have in common, and how do their lessons differ?"

metricdefinition
lesson_recallShare of expected lessons covered by at least one retrieved unit
context_precisionShare of retrieved units that cover an expected lesson
context_purityWord-weighted share of retrieved context inside an expected lesson
citation_accuracyShare of distinct [n] citations whose unit covers an expected lesson
phrase_coverageShare of 3 key phrases present verbatim in the answer
judge_scoreLLM judge (Haiku), not human: answer vs reference answer, 1–5
Covers: a unit overlaps ≥ 50% of the lesson, or ≥ 50% of the unit lies inside it. Precision and purity both rise when fewer units return; purity is the fairer of the two.
Reference answers were written from the transcript, not memory. Expected time ranges come from lessons.json at eval time. Tokens, latency and dollars are logged per call.

6 · Limitations and next steps

The tie is real but small; a held-out set and a human pass come next.

limitationnext step
No held-out set; R tuned on the 12 questionsWrite 12+ held-out questions before viewing v3 retrievals
Judge is Haiku, not humanHuman faithfulness pass on all 24 v3 answers
One 19-minute video; one labelling promptWindowed labelling with overlap; validate across seams
Weak adaptive-k signal: RRF squeezes scores to 0.95–1.0Try raw cosine or smaller RRF k; floor of 2 for enumerations
Stage 2 skipped a fix (q01)Offline check: flag moments near a strong drift dip
Validator gap: a 4-sentence move passes as merge + splitTrack moves and splits separately
Worker scaling: sequential, single-process ingestParallel embedding and labelling workers; write path only
Finding: better units did not raise answer scores on a 19-minute talk, but they cut context by 23% and cost by 20% at zero extra query-time work.
Source: README.md "Limitations and next steps"
Close on the trade: moments bought cleaner, cheaper context without hurting answers, and cost recall. The next experiment that would change my mind is a held-out question set judged by a human.
